Why I built it

Most AI pilots in pharma stall for the same reason. Nobody scored them honestly before the build started. A use case that sounds promising in a workshop turns out to have no way to measure whether its outputs are correct, or it sends patient data to a third-party model, or it automates a decision a regulator expects a person to make.

I wanted a simple way to ask those questions up front, in language a regulated-industry team already uses, and to get back a defensible recommendation instead of a gut call.

What it does

You describe a use case and answer a short set of questions. The tool returns a composite quality score, a clear verdict of Proceed, Pivot, Scale, or Stop, a breakdown across four dimensions, the top risks ranked by severity, a plain-language rationale, and any responsible-AI guardrails that fired.

Four worked examples load with one click, one for each verdict, all drawn from clinical data and digital health: ML-assisted CDISC mapping (Scale), GenAI drafting of data management plans (Proceed), autonomous EDC query resolution (Pivot), and autonomous eligibility screening in a decentralized trial (Stop). You can see how the same engine separates a low-risk internal tool from a high-stakes autonomous one.

Design decisions

I made four choices on purpose, because a tool that scores AI risk should hold itself to the same standard it applies.

It is deterministic.No model judges your use case. The scoring is a fixed rule set, so the same inputs always give the same verdict, and every point traces back to an answer you selected.
The weights are visible and editable.Defaults reflect a regulated posture, with risk and responsible-design weighted higher. You can change them, so the tool imposes no hidden value judgment.
Guardrails can only lower a verdict.A data-protection red line or a missing evaluation method caps the result no matter how strong the rest of the score looks.
It runs entirely in your browser.Nothing you enter leaves the page. No storage, no analytics, no network calls. Privacy by design, not as an optional setting.
How scoring works

Each dimension is scored from its questions on a 0 to 100 scale. The composite is a weighted average of feasibility, evaluation, an inverted risk score where lower risk scores higher, and responsible-by-design. The composite maps to a base verdict by band, and the guardrails can only cap the result lower. The verdict you see is the lower of the base band and any guardrail ceiling.

What the rubric considers

The live tool is a rapid triage. It surfaces the factors that move a verdict the most and applies neutral defaults to the rest, so you get an answer fast. A complete review weighs all of the following, which is the full rubric behind the engine:

FeasibilityProblem definition and success metric, data availability and access, technical approach maturity.
EvaluationWhether an evaluation method exists, and whether there is a ground-truth or expert benchmark to measure against.
Risk and failure modesHallucination and factual error, data leakage, workflow fit and adoption, scalability, vendor overclaim and lock-in, and the consequence if the system is wrong.
Responsible-by-designPrivacy and GDPR handling, auditability and traceability, GxP and regulatory validation fit, human oversight matched to the stakes, and bias and subgroup fairness.

How I built it

I designed the rubric. The dimensions, the questions, the severity levels, the gating logic, and the weighting come from my work in clinical data management and digital health inside GxP-regulated environments. That judgment is the part that matters, and it is mine.

I used AI to generate the implementation. I specified the model, reviewed the logic, and wrote a small test that runs all the examples through the scoring engine to confirm each produces the intended verdict before shipping. The code is AI-assisted. The criteria, the decisions, and the verification are my own. I am stating this plainly because building responsibly with AI includes being honest about how you built it.

What shaped it
A principle from Winning with AI by Charlene Li and Dr. Katia Walsh: most organizations stall running pilots, and the ones that create real value learn to prioritize and govern responsibly. This scorecard is one attempt to put that into practice.

Methodology, frameworks and assumptions

The rubric is informed by recognized responsible-AI frameworks and adapted for clinical and digital-health work: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (its Govern, Map, Measure and Manage functions), the OECD AI Principles, the EU AI Act's risk-tiering logic, Good Machine Learning Practice for medical-device software, and WHO guidance on AI for health. The four dimensions and the guardrails map onto those sources rather than being invented from scratch.

I have tried to state the design assumptions plainly rather than hide them:

Composite scoring is compensatory; guardrails are not.A strength in one dimension can offset a weakness in the blended score, which is why the guardrails sit on top. They enforce non-negotiable floors (data protection, evaluation, autonomy versus stakes, critical risk, fairness) and can only lower a verdict, never raise it.
Guardrails depend only on high-signal inputs.The rapid triage uses neutral defaults for secondary factors, but every guardrail keys off a question the user actually answers, so a fast assessment cannot hide a red line.
The thresholds and weights are conventions, not validated cutoffs.The band boundaries and the dimension weights reflect a regulated-industry posture, with risk and responsible-design weighted higher. They are reasoned defaults, not empirically calibrated, and the weights are visible and adjustable.
It is a self-assessment.One person scoring their own use case will tend to rate it kindly. A second independent reviewer materially reduces that bias, and the tool is designed to start a conversation, not end one.
Limitations and out-of-scope

This is a decision-support aid, not a decision-maker. The verdict reflects what you enter, which is an estimate. It does not replace a Data Protection Impact Assessment, GxP validation, a security review, fairness and subgroup testing, or sign-off from accountable owners. It is not a vendor-ranking or procurement instrument, and it does not assess a specific model's measured performance. Treat the output as a structured starting point for a conversation, not a control.
